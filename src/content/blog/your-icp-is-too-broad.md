Most B2B teams can describe their ICP in about four seconds, because it's been on slide six of the deck since the last planning cycle. "Mid-market manufacturers in Europe, 50 to 500 employees, IT or operations leadership, some digital transformation budget." It comes out sounding confident mainly because it's been rehearsed.

The harder question is the one nobody asks in that meeting: name a company that fits the description perfectly and that you'd still refuse to take a meeting with. What usually follows is a pause, then a weak example, then a change of subject.

That pause is the whole article. Your ICP may well be accurate, but it's inert: it excludes almost nobody, which means it decides almost nothing. A filter that lets everything through is just a description of the market you happen to sell into, with a strategy label stuck on it.

And then everyone wonders why nothing converts.

## An ICP is not a demographic sketch

What most companies have is a firmographic outline: industry, headcount band, geography, maybe a revenue range and a job title. That's the shape of a company, and it tells you nothing about whether the company will buy.

Do the arithmetic on your own version. "Mid-market manufacturers in Europe, 50 to 500 employees" is tens of thousands of organizations. If your team closes thirty deals a year, you've described a population you will never touch and called it a target. The definition is wide enough that every inbound lead technically qualifies, and so does every list a BDR scrapes. So did every deal that drifted for eight months and died in procurement.

What that produces is permission. A broad ICP is how an organization gives itself license to chase anything with a pulse while still feeling disciplined about it, because look, they were in the ICP.

The damage survives because it's never dramatic. A broad ICP produces a pipeline that looks healthy in the CRM, a forecast nobody believes, and a conversion rate that's been mediocre for so long it's now considered the industry benchmark.

## Why your ICP drifts broad

![A large undifferentiated green mass beside one small, sharply defined shape on a lime panel](../../assets/blog/icp-too-broad-01.webp)

Nobody sets out to write a useless ICP. It gets stretched by three pressures, each perfectly reasonable in the moment.

**Fear of shrinking the TAM.** Narrowing the ICP makes the addressable market number in the board deck smaller, and nobody wants to be the person who shrinks that slide. So the definition widens until the TAM looks impressive, and from then on it gets defended for being impressive, whether or not it's true. A big market you can't convert makes a good story for investors and very little else.

**Sales pressure to keep the list long.** Reps need coverage, a tight ICP means a short list, and a short list feels like a quota problem waiting to happen. So the boundaries loosen until the list is long enough to feel safe. And the long list is exactly what makes the quota harder, because the same selling hours now spread across four times the accounts, most of which were never going to buy.

**It was written once and never tested.** This is the big one. The ICP was defined in a strategy session, early, based on who the founders believed would buy. Since then you've closed and lost real deals and churned real customers, and all of that is evidence. Almost nobody goes back and checks the definition against it. The deck says one thing, the CRM says another, and the two documents have never been in the same room.

Ask yourself when your ICP was last edited because of something that actually happened. If the answer is "never," what you have is a founding assumption with impressive tenure.

## What a real ICP is made of

### Evidence from your best accounts

Pull your best twenty accounts, and be strict about what best means: they closed fast, at a margin you didn't have to gut, and then stayed, all three at once. Logo size doesn't enter into it, and neither does how often the CRO likes to name-drop them.

Now look for what they share that *isn't* firmographic, which is usually something structural. There was already a person whose job description covered the thing you fix. The company had been burned by a previous attempt at solving it, and it had a deadline coming at it from outside. That's the actual profile, and the industry and headcount were coincidence.

### A buying trigger

Firmographics tell you who *could* buy. Triggers tell you who buys *now*. A company that fits your profile perfectly but has no reason to move this year goes in your someday file for now, and someday isn't a sales stage.

Triggers are events with a date attached: new leadership in the function you sell to, an acquisition that broke the reporting, a regulation with a compliance deadline, a system going end-of-life, a failed audit. Fit without a trigger is a nurture sequence, and a trigger without fit is a distraction that will eat a quarter. You need both before it's a deal.

### An explicit list of who doesn't fit

The ICP isn't finished until it names, in writing, the companies you turn away.

"We're wrong for organizations with no internal owner for this." "We're wrong for anyone who wants a single vendor for everything." "We're wrong below 50 employees, whatever the budget, because there's nobody there to run the change once we leave."

That list belongs in the same document, in plain language, where a rep can read it in the thirty seconds before deciding to spend the next four months on a deal.

## The disqualification test

To find out fast whether you have an ICP or a mood board, hand it to your newest rep. Give them a company (website, LinkedIn, a bit of context) and ask them to say no in thirty seconds using nothing but the ICP.

If they can't, it isn't an ICP.

A definition that can only ever confirm is a comfort object. A filter's value lies entirely in what it rejects, because everything it lets through you now have to pay for with someone's calendar.

And look at the asymmetry people keep getting backwards. A bad *yes* costs months of two people's time, a demo, a security review, a proposal, five follow-ups, and a slot in the forecast that poisons the number. A bad *no* costs you a company you might have won, which is real, but you find out fast and it's cheap. Most teams behave as though the second is the expensive mistake. It's almost always the first.

## How to sharpen it

![A scattered cluster of abstract shapes cut by a diagonal line, lime on one side and forest green on the other](../../assets/blog/icp-too-broad-02.webp)

Four moves, none of which need a workshop.

**Compare closed-won against churned and lost.** Put the three lists side by side. Most teams only ever study their wins, which is like studying survivors and concluding that nothing is dangerous. Wins tell you what worked. Churn and losses tell you what your ICP can't see, and your disqualifiers are hiding in those second and third columns.

**Find the disqualifiers.** Look at your worst outcomes: the deals that dragged forever, the customers who left in year one, the accounts that consume support like they're paying five times what they pay. There's a pattern, and it's almost never firmographic. The usual suspects are a missing internal owner, a purchase pushed from above with no real pain behind it, and a team that wanted a tool when it needed a process. Whatever it is, that's a disqualifier, and it's worth more than any qualifier you have.

**Add the timing trigger.** Go back to your wins and ask one question: what changed the month they started looking? You're after whatever started the clock, which is a separate question from why they picked you. The answer tells you which accounts deserve a rep's time this quarter and which are three years early. Write down the top three triggers and make them part of the definition.

**Re-test every quarter.** Evidence keeps accumulating whether you look at it or not, so the ICP should have a version history. If your product has changed in two years and your ICP hasn't, one of them is lying to you. That quarterly check of your beliefs against your own data is the core of [go-to-market](/services/go-to-market) work done properly, even if it's far less fun than the strategy offsite.

## A narrower ICP doesn't shrink your pipeline. It stops you wasting it.

The objection arrives on schedule: "if we narrow this, we'll have less pipeline."

You'll have less pipeline *in the CRM*. You'll have the same revenue or more, and you'll get to it faster, because the pipeline you're cutting was never going to close. It was going to sit in stage two for five months while a rep sent polite follow-ups into a void, and then get marked closed-lost in an end-of-quarter cleanup that everyone agrees not to talk about.

A sharp ICP takes away the fake deals, which feel exactly like real ones right up until the last week of the quarter.

What you get back is concentration: fewer accounts, worked properly, by a team that stops being thinly spread across an entire market and gets disproportionately good at one slice of it. Every conversation gets sharper because you've had the same conversation forty times. That's what the narrow definition buys: a business that compounds.

So run the test. Hand your ICP to someone who joined last month and ask them to reject a company with it in thirty seconds. If they can't, you've found what's been capping your conversion rate all along, and it's more basic than the messaging, the website, or the budget: you never decided who this is for.
