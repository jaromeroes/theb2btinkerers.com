Open your homepage and read the first thing above the fold. If it's a version of "the leading platform for [category] with AI-powered [capability]," I already know something about your pipeline: it's harder to fill than it should be, because nobody has ever bought a product. People buy the problem it makes go away, and right now your messaging is making them do the translation work themselves.

This is the most expensive mistake in B2B marketing, and the hardest to spot. It has nothing to do with bad design or a small budget. The site is perfectly competent, talks fluently about itself, and says almost nothing about the person reading it.

## The mirror problem

Most B2B companies build their marketing like a mirror that reflects the product back at whoever's looking: features, specs, modules, the word "platform" three times per paragraph, a capabilities grid, an integrations list. It's all technically true, and all of it is centered on the seller.

The buyer walks in carrying a problem, maybe a number they're behind on, a process that keeps breaking, or a boss asking why last quarter slipped. They look at your mirror and see... your product. Nothing in it shows them their own situation or the thing keeping them up at night. So they do a quick, mostly unconscious calculation: "Is this relevant to me?" And because you made them work to answer that, most of them decide it isn't and leave.

You lost that deal on messaging, and your product may well have been the better one. You described the thing you sell while they came to solve a problem, and the gap between those two messages is where deals die, with the buyer never telling you why.

## People don't want a drill. They want a hole.

Theodore Levitt said it decades ago and it still gets misquoted in every pitch deck: people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole. Clayton Christensen took the idea further with Jobs-to-be-Done. His frame is that customers don't really "buy" products. They "hire" them to make progress in a specific situation. The job is the thing they're trying to get done, and the product is just the current best candidate for it.

In B2B this is even more true, because nobody is buying with their own money and everyone is buying with their own reputation. Reframe the drill for a second. The person evaluating your data platform wants to stop being the department that can't answer the CEO's question by Friday. Whoever buys your security tool wants to stop lying awake about the breach that ends their career. And your automation suite is for someone whose team is drowning, who needs the strategic project to finally ship.

The job is emotional and social as much as functional. The practical outcome matters, but so do the risk of choosing wrong, the status that comes with looking competent to the board, and the relief when a problem is finally handled. Messaging that speaks only to the functional layer addresses maybe a third of what's driving the decision.

This also changes what your product is. Different buyers can hire the same tool for completely different jobs, and the message that wins names the job. A reporting product hired to survive the board meeting makes a different promise from the same product hired to catch fraud faster, even though the feature list on both pages would be identical. Leading with the product flattens all of those jobs into one generic pitch that fits none of them well. Lead with the job instead and you suddenly sound like you were built for exactly the person reading.

## Why feature-led marketing fails

Feature-led messaging fails for two reasons, and both are boring, which is exactly why they go unfixed for years.

The first is translation cost. When you lead with features, you're handing the buyer homework: "Here are our capabilities, now figure out which of your problems they solve." Some buyers, the ones already deep in evaluation, will do that work. The vast majority, the ones still deciding whether you're even worth a demo, won't. They bounce before deciding whether you could help, because you asked them to prove it themselves and they had eleven other tabs open.

The second is interchangeability. Define yourself by your feature list and you've volunteered to be compared line by line against every competitor who lists the same features. You become a row in a spreadsheet, and on a spreadsheet the only axis anyone can read is price. The closer your capabilities look to everyone else's, the more the decision collapses onto that one number, which is the last place you want it. Problem-led messaging gets you out of the spreadsheet, because the way you frame the job, the outcome, and the stakes is much harder to copy than a checkbox.

## How to find the real job

To find the real job, skip the positioning workshop where your own team guesses on sticky notes, and talk to people who recently bought, ideally in the awkward weeks right after they signed, while the memory is fresh.

Ask them about the switch. What was happening the week they decided to look for something? There's always a trigger: a miss, a scare, a new mandate, a competitor doing something that made the status quo feel dangerous. That moment tells you the real job. Ask "why did you buy" and you'll get the rationalized version instead.

Then map the forces, which is the underused half of Jobs-to-be-Done. Two things pull them toward change: the push of their current pain and the pull of your solution. Two others hold them back: their anxiety about the new thing and their attachment to the old, familiar mess. Good messaging turns up the first two and defuses the last two. Most B2B copy only ever pushes and never addresses the fear of switching, which is usually why deals stall.

![Two colleagues in a bright meeting room leaning over a notebook together, one gesturing while the other listens intently, mid-conversation](../../assets/blog/buy-the-problem-02.webp)

Ask what "good" looked like to them, and push past the feature they wanted to the outcome that would let them exhale. When someone says "I just needed to walk into the QBR without getting ambushed," that sentence is worth more than your entire feature matrix. It's the job, and it's your headline. Write it down verbatim, because your customer just wrote your homepage for you and did it better than any copywriter would.

Then rewrite. Lead with the problem and the outcome, so the buyer sees themselves in the first line, recognizes their own situation, and feels understood before you've asked them for anything. Only then should the product show up, as the answer to a question you've already made them feel. The features stay, but they move to where they belong. They become proof, and they arrive after the buyer already wants the outcome.

## Sell the hole

The reframe is simple to say and hard to do, because everyone in your building is in love with the drill. You built it and you know every setting, so of course you want to talk about it. But the buyer is standing in front of a wall thinking about the hole, and every sentence you spend admiring your own craftsmanship is a sentence that doesn't reach them.

Go read your own homepage one more time with a single question in mind: does the first thing a stranger sees describe my product, or their problem? If it's the product, you've built a mirror. Turn it into a window, one they can look through and see their own situation on the other side, with your product standing next to the outcome they wanted all along.

Shifting from what you made to what it makes possible is how you stop being a line on a spreadsheet and become the obvious answer to a question the buyer already can't stop asking.
