Open next year's marketing budget and find the line for the trade show. You know the one: same hall, same week of the calendar, same booth footprint, roughly the same number with a bit added for inflation. Now answer honestly: who decided to go?

Not who approved it. Who *decided*.

Most likely nobody did, not this year anyway. You're going because you went last year, and last year you went because you went the year before. Somewhere back at the origin, a real person had a real reason that made sense at the time, and then nobody examined it again. What started as a decision turned into a ritual. That ritual is frequently the single largest line item in the B2B marketing budget, and reliably the one with the least thinking behind it.

Some B2B events work extraordinarily well, and I'll make that case properly further down. Most companies, though, never decide to attend in the first place; they just renew.

## The ritual nobody re-decides

Watch how the trade show enters the plan. It never gets proposed, debated and won, because it's already there. It arrives in the budget template as a pre-existing fact, and the only conversation anyone has about it is logistical: which hall, how big a stand, who's flying out, whether we do the sponsored session again. Nobody asks whether to go at all.

That's what separates the event line from everything else in your plan. A new campaign has to justify itself: someone has to stand up, make a case, and survive the questions. The trade show skips all of that because everyone treats it as a renewal, and renewals get processed without anyone interrogating them. Your finance team will scrutinize a $15,000 (€14,000) content project harder than a six-figure hall you've booked eight years running, purely because one is new and the other is already on the shelf.

The tell is what happens when you ask the team why you go. The answers all look backward: "Our customers are there." "We've always had a presence." "It'd look strange if we didn't show up." Each may even be true, and none of them is a hypothesis. They're descriptions of the past being used as a plan for the future.

## Why it survives every budget cut

When a bad quarter forces cuts, the event line is almost never the one that goes, and that should bother you. Content gets trimmed, the paid test gets paused and headcount gets frozen while the booth survives.

There are three reasons, and none of them are about performance.

**It's visible.** The CEO walks the booth, shakes hands, sees a crowded stand and feels the energy of the room. It's a vivid, physical experience of marketing existing, and no dashboard competes with that. A nurture sequence moving twelve accounts toward a decision produces no such moment, which is exactly why it gets cut first.

**It feels like activity.** There's a stand to build, a schedule to run, badges to print, a team in motion. It generates enormous visible effort, and visible effort reads as productivity. Nobody looks at a team frantically prepping a booth and thinks "is this the highest-value use of the quarter?"

**Its returns are conveniently unmeasurable.** This reason carries the most weight. When you ask what the show produced, the answer arrives in a language that can't be checked: brand, presence, relationships, being seen in the market. Those things are real, and brand-building compounds; I'd defend that in any room. But the claim is built so it can never be wrong. If pipeline shows up, the show worked. If it doesn't, the show was building brand and relationships that will pay off eventually.

Unfalsifiable spending is the safest spending in the building, because what can't be tested can't be attacked. That safety is precisely the problem: "can't be proven wrong" is doing an awful lot of work in a budget that's supposed to be allocating finite money to its best use.

## The arithmetic nobody runs

![Overhead view of a vast, mostly empty exhibition hall at dawn before the crowds arrive](../../assets/blog/trade-shows-01.webp)

Take one mid-sized company at one industry show and do the honest math. It's an illustrative example, with no research or benchmark behind it, and your numbers will differ, so run yours.

Booth space, a modest stand at a decent industry event: $30,000 (€27,000). Design, build, furniture, shipping in, shipping out: $35,000 (€32,000). Flights, hotels, per diems for eight people over four days: $20,000 (€18,000). Collateral, giveaways, the good coffee machine, the sponsored session upgrade: $15,000 (€14,000).

Subtotal: $100,000 (€91,000). That's the number in the budget line, and it's already the biggest thing on the page.

Now add what the budget line never shows. Eight people are off their normal work for a week, and two of them are your best AEs, who are not touching pipeline while they're standing on carpet. Realistically there's also a week of prep before and a week of follow-up after, spread across a smaller group. Load those salaries properly and you're adding roughly $30,000 (€27,000) in people cost that appears nowhere in the event budget.

So the full cost is around $130,000 (€118,000). Then there's the line that never gets written down at all: what that same money and that same week of your best people would have produced concentrated on one focused motion for a quarter. Skipping that comparison is how the habit stays alive.

Against that, what comes back? Usually a spreadsheet of badge scans, and badge scans are not pipeline. A badge scan is a record that a human walked within a meter of a device, and it tells you nothing about intent, need, budget or timeline. Counting them and calling it a result is how a $130,000 (€118,000) decision gets reported as a success without anyone lying.

## The case for events is real

![A close, quiet conversation between two people at a small table away from a busy hall](../../assets/blog/trade-shows-02.webp)

Now the fair part I promised at the start. Events do something no other channel does: they compress into three days a set of conversations that would take nine months to arrange over video, with people who are physically present, out of their inbox, and in a mood to talk. When the goal is specific, the return can be outstanding.

There are three cases where I'd argue *for* the spend without hesitation. When you have a named target-account list and the buying committee is walking that floor, an event is the cheapest way to get in front of people who won't take your call. When you're launching, and the press, analysts, and partners who shape your category are concentrated in one building for seventy-two hours, that concentration is worth the money. And when you need deep customer research, the kind you can't get on a scheduled call because people are performing, the hallway conversation at hour six of day two will tell you more about your market than a quarter of survey data.

All three have a defined goal, a named audience, and a result you'd recognize if you got it. The failure comes from going without a hypothesis and then reverse-engineering a justification from whatever happened to occur.

## Decide before you book

The fix is unglamorous and takes about an hour. Do it before the deposit, because once the deposit is paid, any "decision" is a rationalization.

**Write the hypothesis.** One sentence, in the same format you'd demand of any campaign: "We're going because we believe X will happen, and it matters because Y." If you can't write that sentence, the trip is a habit with a booking reference.

**Write the disqualifier.** Everybody skips this one, and it's what makes the exercise real. Before you book, define what would tell you this was the wrong call. If no evidence could ever count against going, you're back in unfalsifiable territory and you haven't made a decision at all.

**Pre-book meetings with named accounts, or don't go.** This is the sharpest test available. Set a floor, "twelve confirmed meetings with accounts from the target list before we commit to the stand," and work to hit it. If you can't get twelve target accounts to agree to twenty minutes when they're already going to be in the building with nothing to lose, the market is telling you something about your relevance, and that answer is worth far more than the show.

**Measure the things that can be wrong.** That means meetings held with target accounts, opportunities created or advanced, and pipeline influenced within a defined window. Badge scans, booth traffic and "great energy on the stand" don't count. Pick metrics that could come back bad, because a metric that can only come back good isn't measuring anything. If you're not sure your current numbers survive that test, that's usually a sign the whole spend map needs [a proper audit](/services/marketing-audit), and the event line is only one part of it.

## You never decided

The question was never whether trade shows work. Plenty of them do, for companies that turned up with a purpose and left with the thing they came for. The question is whether you ever decided to go, or whether you inherited an obligation from a version of the company that made the call under conditions that no longer apply.

Renewing is what happens when nobody decides, and it looks enough like a decision to pass for one. The most expensive habits are the ones you can afford, which is exactly why nobody ever stops to check.

So this year, before the deposit goes out, ask the question that hasn't been asked in eight years: if this weren't already in the budget, would we put it there?
