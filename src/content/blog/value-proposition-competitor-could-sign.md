Try this test on your own website. Copy your value proposition into a document, paste the value propositions of your three closest competitors right underneath, and strip out the logos. Now read all four and try to tell them apart.

If you can't, you have the most expensive problem in B2B, because it's the one nobody notices.

The next test is sharper than any positioning workshop. Take your homepage headline and imagine your biggest competitor putting their logo on it. Would anything become false? Would anyone at their company object? If the answer is no, and a rival could sign your value proposition and mean every word, then what you have is positioning theater: something that looks like a stance while committing to nothing.

## The sameness test most B2B companies fail

"Trusted partner." "End-to-end solutions." "Innovative, customer-centric, results-driven." Read enough B2B homepages and they blur into one long sentence written by a committee that has never met a specific customer.

The words feel safe because they're true. You *are* trusted, and you *do* deliver solutions. The trouble is that every claim on that list is true for your competitors too, and a statement that applies equally to everyone in your category carries exactly zero information for the buyer trying to choose.

Worse, this sameness is the predictable result of how most companies make positioning decisions, which is why better copywriting doesn't cure it. The blandness on the page is a symptom of something further upstream.

## Why it happens: four forces behind the sameness

![Four abstract shapes converging into one identical form](../../assets/blog/value-proposition-01.webp)

Nobody sits in a meeting and says "let's sound exactly like everyone else." Sameness gets manufactured by four forces, each one reasonable on its own.

**The fear of narrowing.** Choosing who you're *for* means choosing who you're *not* for, and that feels like leaving money on the table. So the value proposition gets stretched wide enough to welcome everyone. A message built to exclude nobody ends up meaning nothing to anybody, and the wider it gets, the harder it is to remember.

**Committee wording.** Positioning by consensus is a machine for removing edges. Sales wants it broader so no deal feels off-limits. Legal wants the bold claim softened, and a VP wants their pet segment included. Every round of feedback sands off another sharp corner, and what's left is the smooth, inoffensive average that survives precisely *because* it offends no internal stakeholder. The problem is that internal stakeholders aren't your buyers.

**Copying the category leader.** When one company defines the category, everyone else instinctively echoes their language to seem credible, to signal "we play in this space too." Echoing the leader confirms you're the alternative nobody had to consider, and none of the leader's authority rubs off on you for using their words.

**Confusing "what we do" with "why we're the only sane choice."** Most value propositions describe a category. "We help B2B companies grow pipeline" is what you do, and it gives nobody a reason to pick you over the eleven other firms that say the identical thing. Describing your function is table stakes, and positioning starts where that description ends.

## What real positioning requires

Most teams flinch from the idea that positioning is a set of trade-offs rather than a list of strengths.

Trade-offs are the foundation of strategy. Michael Porter has argued for decades that the essence of strategy is choosing what *not* to do. A position is defined as much by what it rejects as by what it claims. If your value proposition has no downside, if there's no type of customer it deliberately turns away, then what you've written is a wish.

Real positioning means being explicitly wrong for some buyers so you can be obviously right for others. The premium option is wrong for the price-sensitive buyer, on purpose, and the specialist is wrong for the company that wants one vendor for everything, also on purpose. That "wrongness" is the proof that a position exists at all.

The practical test: does your value proposition contain a claim someone could reasonably disagree with, a point of view a competitor might actively reject? If everyone in your market would agree with it, you've described a consensus, and nobody notices a consensus. Specificity beats safety, because a specific claim is the only kind a buyer can remember and repeat to a colleague.

## A practical path out of sameness

![A single distinct shape standing apart from a cluster of identical ones](../../assets/blog/value-proposition-02.webp)

The way out is to make the hard choices the committee has been avoiding, and a thesaurus and a few better adjectives won't make them for you. It takes four moves.

**Name who you're *not* for.** Write it down, literally: "This is wrong for companies that want X." The moment you can name who you turn away, the people you're *for* recognize themselves instantly, and the message starts to feel written for them.

**Name the enemy.** Pick the status quo you're replacing, the broken default your buyer tolerates today, and leave competitors out of it. Great positioning always has a villain: the bloated incumbent, the "we've always done it this way," the expensive tool nobody uses. Naming what you're against tells buyers what you're for, faster than any feature list.

**Claim one thing you'd stake the business on.** Narrow five differentiators down to one: the single conviction you'd defend when a prospect pushes back, the thing you believe that your competitors either don't believe or won't say publicly. If you can't reduce it to one sentence you'd bet on, you haven't found it yet.

**Pressure-test every line with the signature test.** For each claim on your homepage, ask: could a competitor say this too, and mean it? If yes, cut it or sharpen it until the answer is no. Run the whole page through that filter and watch how much "safe" copy turns out to be dead weight.

## Differentiation is the willingness to be disliked

This is what makes people nervous: you have to be willing to be disliked by the wrong customers.

A value proposition that everyone finds agreeable is one that nobody finds compelling. Once your positioning is sharp enough to make the wrong-fit buyer think "that's not for me," it is sharp enough to make the right-fit buyer think "finally, someone gets it." You can't have the second reaction without accepting the first, because both come from the same edge.

So go back to the test and put your value proposition next to your competitors'. If they could sign it, it was never yours to begin with. The goal is to be undeniable to a specific slice of the market and deliberately wrong for the rest, which means giving up on being liked by all of it.
