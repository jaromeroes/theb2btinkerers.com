Fewer campaigns, more impact. It sounds like a poster on a wall nobody reads. But look at your plan for this quarter. If you're running eight campaigns at once and can't name which two actually matter, you already know the problem. The team is busy and the calendar is full, but almost nothing is breaking through.

This is the spray reflex, and most B2B marketing teams have it bad. Spraying feels safe, because saying no to a campaign is like leaving pipeline on the table, and being busy across everything passes for insurance. So you keep a little of everything alive, all the time: the webinar series, the paid social test, the ABM pilot, the newsletter refresh, the partner co-marketing thing, the event, the two content pushes. What you end up with is a thin layer of mediocre everywhere and nothing that lands.

## How the spray reflex builds up

The logic behind spraying is emotional. Nobody sits down and decides "let's run eight half-committed campaigns." The campaigns just accumulate: someone had an idea, a stakeholder asked, a channel looked promising, a competitor did a thing. Each addition felt reasonable on its own, and nobody ever subtracts.

So the portfolio grows, and somewhere along the way the reasoning flips. The question stops being "what's the smartest use of our capacity this quarter" and becomes "what can we keep alive without dropping any of it." At that point you have a to-do list with "strategy" written at the top.

The tell is when you ask the team what's working and everyone gets vague, even though nobody is hiding anything. None of the eight has had enough runway to produce a clear signal, and you can't read results from a campaign you ran once, half-funded, between four other things.

## Why concentration actually wins

![A single spotlight beam cutting through darkness onto one figure while faint scattered lights fade around it](../../assets/blog/fewer-campaigns-01.webp)

Attention, budget, and team focus are all finite. Your hours and your spend are fixed, and so is the team's creative and analytical horsepower. When you split those across eight campaigns, each one gets one-eighth of everything. It gets one-eighth of the budget to buy noticeability and one-eighth of the team's attention to iterate on it, and the team gets one-eighth of the reps it would need to get good at running it.

And most channels have a threshold. Below a certain level of spend, frequency, and consistency, a campaign simply doesn't register. Your buyers don't see it often enough to remember it, and the algorithm never gets enough signal to optimize. Meanwhile, the team doesn't run it enough times to learn what works. You're paying for the campaign without buying the outcome, because you never crossed the line where it starts to compound.

Fewer campaigns, with more behind each one, clear that threshold. Concentrate the same budget into two campaigns instead of eight and each one now has the spend to get seen and the runway to iterate, with enough of the team's brain on it to improve it week over week.

A team spread this thin generates a lot of motion and not much progress. If your team is stuck in reactive mode and everything already feels urgent, the [operating-system problem](/insights) usually sits underneath the portfolio problem, and it's worth fixing both.

## A method for deciding what to cut

Concentration only works if you can decide what to concentrate on. And "trust your gut" doesn't survive contact with a room full of stakeholders who each sponsor a campaign. You need a method that's simple enough that people will use it and hard enough to force a choice.

Score every candidate campaign on three things.

**ICP fit.** Does this campaign target the accounts and buyers you want, or the ones that happen to be easy to reach? A campaign that generates volume from outside your ideal customer profile is worse than no campaign, because it fills the funnel with people sales will resent chasing. Score honestly: "might attract some good-fit accounts" is a no.

**A written pipeline hypothesis.** This is the one that separates real campaigns from activity. For each candidate, write one sentence: "We believe this campaign will produce X because Y." If you can't write it, what you have is a vibe. Compare "Run more LinkedIn ads" with "We believe a founder-led LinkedIn series will generate qualified demo requests from mid-market ops leaders, because that segment engages with peer expertise over gated PDFs." Only the second is a hypothesis. It's testable, and it tells you what to measure.

**Effort versus impact.** Not everything worth doing is worth doing now. Plot each candidate on how much it'll cost in team hours and spend against how much pipeline it could realistically move. High-impact, low-effort ones are obvious yeses. The high-effort, high-impact ones are your bigger bets, and you can afford maybe one or two of them. Anything low-impact dies, no matter how much someone loves it.

Then apply the rule that makes all of this work: kill or park anything without a clear hypothesis, and don't let "keep it small" sneak in as a third option. A parked campaign waits for a quarter where it's the priority. After that, sequence whatever survives. Three campaigns run one after another, each with full focus, will out-produce six run simultaneously at half strength, and most teams never think about the order at all.

## The hard part isn't the spreadsheet

![Two hands across a table, one sliding a card away into a discard pile while the other reaches to hold it back](../../assets/blog/fewer-campaigns-02.webp)

You could build the scoring model in an afternoon. What stops teams is that cutting a campaign is a political act.

Every campaign on that list has a sponsor. Someone proposed the webinar series. Someone's quarterly goal is tied to the partner program. The loudest request often comes from the most senior person in the room, and "not this quarter" is a hard sentence to say to a VP who's personally attached to their idea. So teams keep everything, whatever the math says, because subtracting means a conversation nobody wants to have.

That conversation becomes survivable once the trade-off is visible, because that turns a personal favor into a business decision. Don't argue about whether someone's campaign is good; almost every campaign is defensible in isolation. Put them all on one surface, with the scores and the finite budget in plain view, and ask a different question: "which two of these do we fund with the capacity we actually have?"

When the cost of a yes is visible, the conversation changes. You're now the person showing that the team has room for two bets, and the group has to choose which two, so no idea dies on your say-so alone. The cut becomes a prioritization, and prioritization is a decision leadership is supposed to make. Protect the focus from the loudest request by making loudness irrelevant.

This is also where you protect the strategic work. The campaigns tied to this quarter's real pipeline target rarely have the angriest sponsor. Make them visible and scored alongside everything else, and they stop getting bumped without anyone noticing.

## Focus is the multiplier nobody puts on the plan

Every marketing plan lists channels, budgets, timelines, and targets. Almost none of them list focus as a line item, even though it's the thing that determines whether any of the rest works. You can have the right channels and the right budget and still get nothing, because you spread both so thin that neither crossed the threshold of mattering.

Fewer campaigns, more impact isn't about doing less work. It's about arranging the same finite resources so they break through. Spread across eight campaigns, they dissolve into a background hum nobody notices. Two campaigns done fully will beat eight done partially, almost every time, and the win comes from deciding, in advance and in front of everyone, what you won't do. That's the step most teams skip.
