The most expensive assumption in B2B marketing is that the people you're selling to are ready to buy. Almost none of them are, and the whole machinery of lead-gen is built as if they were. Brand-building is the part of marketing that accepts the opposite (most of your future customers aren't in the market today) and does something about it anyway. You can't see it on this quarter's dashboard, but over any horizon that matters it's the work that wins. Whether you believe that decides where your budget goes.

## The 95% you're pretending don't exist

There's a finding from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, put clearly by John Dawes, that should reframe how you think about every campaign you run. It's called the 95-5 rule. At any given moment, only about 5% of B2B buyers are in-market, meaning they have a live need, a budget, and an intention to buy something in your category. The other 95% are running their business and not thinking about you at all, let alone evaluating vendors or filling out forms.

Now look at where most B2B marketing money goes. It funds demand capture (paid search on high-intent keywords, retargeting, gated content designed to catch a hand-raise) and sales-led nurture sequences that fire the moment someone downloads a whitepaper. All of it is aimed, with great precision, at that 5%, where it competes head to head with every other vendor doing exactly the same thing to the same tiny pool.

This is the uncomfortable math nobody brings up in the QBR. You can have the sharpest lead-gen engine on earth, and it still can't convert someone who isn't buying. A demo won't turn a 95%-er into a customer this quarter, and neither will a discount or a sequence of seven perfectly timed emails. They aren't resisting your pitch. They just aren't in the market, and pushing harder doesn't shorten a sales cycle that hasn't started.

## What brand does that lead-gen structurally cannot

So what do you do with 95% of your market who can't be converted today? You could ignore them, which is what most budgets do by default. Or you could make sure that when they *do* enter the market, next quarter or next year, you're already the name in their head.

That's the entire job of brand-building: the slow, deliberate construction of memory in people who aren't ready yet, so that at the exact moment a need appears, you're the first company they think of and the easiest one to justify choosing. Awareness for its own sake won't do that, and neither will logo impressions you feel good about. When the need hits, buyers pull from a mental shortlist they've been assembling, half-consciously, for months or years. Brand-building is how you get on that list before the buying even starts.

This is the marketing you can't see. Its output today is a bias, a slight tilt toward you, in a stranger who won't act for eighteen months. There's no lead to report, which is exactly why it's so easy to defund, and it's also why the companies that hold their nerve on it pull away from the ones that don't. The effect compounds: every quarter you build memory in the 95% is a quarter of future pipeline you're pre-loading, and it accumulates in a way that lead-gen spend, which resets to zero the day you turn it off, never does.

## Why the wrong thing wins the argument anyway

If brand-building is so powerful, why does it lose the budget fight almost every time? It comes down to one brutal asymmetry: lead-gen attributes cleanly and brand doesn't.

You can draw a straight line from a paid search click to a form fill to a closed deal. It shows up in the dashboard, with a CPA, a ROAS, and a number your CFO can point at. Brand-building has none of that. Its payoff is diffuse, delayed, and tangled up with everything else that happened over the same period. You cannot prove that the deal you closed today came from the memory you built two years ago, even when it did. So in every budget conversation the measurable thing beats the important thing, because it's easier to defend.

This is the tyranny of last-click, and it warps the whole function. Marketers optimize for what they can report, which pushes spend relentlessly toward the bottom of the funnel, where the 5% are and where the metrics light up a slide. The strategic work, the brand-building that would grow the business, has no angry attribution model demanding it happen. So it gets cut first whenever the quarter looks tight, because it's important but never provable. If that pattern sounds familiar, it's the same short-termism we've written about in [why your marketing team is always firefighting](/insights): the measurable, urgent thing crowds out the important one.

## You need brand and lead-gen, in a ratio you choose

The 95-5 rule tempts people to overcorrect. They get religion about brand and swing to the other extreme, all long-term and no capture, which is just as broken. You still need to convert the 5% who are in-market right now, or you're building memory you never cash in.

The most useful guidance we have comes from Les Binet and Peter Field, who spent years analyzing what drives long-term growth versus short-term response. Their finding, roughly, is that the optimal split is around 60% of budget on brand-building and 40% on activation, on the lead-gen and demand capture that convert existing intent. Brand builds the demand; activation harvests it. Starve either side and the machine stalls.

Now, be honest about your own situation before you apply that number. The 60/40 benchmark comes largely from big-budget, established brands, and if you're a smaller B2B company still fighting to be known at all, your reality is different. You may need more weight on capture in the near term just to stay afloat and keep the pipeline moving, and that's fine. You don't have to copy the ratio to the decimal, but you do need to *have* one, chosen deliberately and out loud. Without one, last-click attribution will drag you to 95% activation and 5% brand without anyone ever deciding that on purpose. Pick your split, defend it, and hold it through the quarters when the dashboard begs you to raid the brand budget for one more capture push.

Brand-building only compounds if you have the patience to let it run long enough. Fund it for a quarter, panic when it doesn't produce leads, and kill it, and you've proven nothing except that you didn't understand what you bought.

## The bet worth making

The best marketing you'll ever do is a bet on a buyer who isn't ready yet. It won't show up in this quarter's numbers or have a clean CPA, and to anyone watching only the dashboard it'll look like money that didn't do anything. Then, eighteen months from now, a stranger who never filled out a form will put you on their shortlist first and won't quite be able to tell you why.

That stranger is where durable growth comes from. Lead-gen fishes the 5% everyone else is also fishing, at a cost you pay every quarter, forever. Brand-building reaches the 95% before they start looking and becomes an asset you build once and keep drawing on.
