There is a specific kind of meeting that marketing leaders are having more and more often. Someone from finance, or sales, or the CEO's office shares something they built over a weekend: a full competitive analysis, a product launch brief, or a content calendar for the next quarter. It looks complete, with structure, headings, and bullet points, and it took them maybe forty minutes.

Then comes the implication, sometimes stated and sometimes not: why does your team need three weeks for something like this?

That is the AI slop problem, and it is not going away.

## What AI slop is

The term is blunt but accurate. AI slop is content or strategic output that looks finished but isn't, with the shape of thinking and none of the substance. It answers the question exactly as it was asked, even when that was the wrong question. It sounds confident and fluent, and it is frequently wrong in ways that are hard to spot unless you already know the answer.

The danger is that it looks good enough to someone who doesn't know what good looks like in your specific context: your market, your positioning, your customers, your sales cycle, your brand voice built over years of iteration.

A competitive analysis generated in forty minutes will identify the right competitors and describe their products accurately. It will probably miss what your Sales Director learned last quarter from three lost deals. It won't capture the positioning nuance that took your team two years to understand, or reflect the segment you are actually targeting as opposed to the one your competitors talk about publicly.

## Why this is a marketing problem specifically

AI is affecting every function in a company. Finance teams use it, and so do product and operations. But most of what they produce can be checked against data, or it sits inside a workflow that already has quality control.

Marketing output is different. A financial model either reconciles or it doesn't, and a legal brief cites the right precedents or it doesn't. With a piece of positioning, a brand narrative, or a campaign concept, quality is much harder to judge without deep context. So the slop is harder to catch, and the bar gets set by whoever produces something first.

If a non-marketer produces a positioning document in forty minutes and presents it in a leadership meeting, it becomes the reference point. From then on your team has to argue against something that already exists, with no chance to build from first principles, and that structural disadvantage compounds over time.

## What it costs you is coherence

Individual AI-generated assets can be good. A well-prompted brief, a first draft of a product page, or a framework for segmenting a new market are legitimate uses of the technology, and they do speed up execution when a trained marketer is in the loop. The problem is coherence across all of them.

A style guide captures only part of a brand voice. The voice itself is a consistent set of choices about what you say, how you say it, what you leave unsaid, and why. It gets built through hundreds of small decisions by people who understand the brand deeply. Give an AI tool the style guide and it will approximate that voice. It won't keep that voice intact when a new product brief pushes against it or a regional team wants to adapt the messaging for a different market, and it won't say no when the Sales Director asks for a one-pager that sounds more aggressive.

Coherence takes judgment, and judgment depends on context that can't be fully encoded in a prompt. Add up many individually acceptable AI outputs, each one prompted by a different person in a different context, and you get a brand that slowly stops sounding like itself. That is already happening in most mid-market B2B companies that have given their teams broad access to AI tools without a framework for using them.

## What the marketing team's job is

The conversation about AI in marketing tends to focus on speed and volume: how many assets you can produce, how fast a brief turns into a campaign, and what each piece of content ends up costing. Those are real efficiency gains, but all of them are about inputs.

The job of a B2B marketing team is not to produce assets. It is to build a consistent, credible presence in a market, one that moves a specific set of buyers from awareness to consideration to preference to purchase. That takes a through-line of thinking that connects every asset to every other asset, and connects all of them to a commercial objective.

AI speeds up execution, but someone still has to decide what to execute, in what order, for whom, with what message, and why.

The teams that will use AI well are the ones investing in that thinking first and using AI to scale the execution. Teams that invert the order, producing output fast and then looking for the strategy inside it, will struggle.

## What to do about it

Keep using AI, with a clear understanding of what it can and cannot hold. In practice, three things help.

**Define what requires human judgment before you start.** Positioning, messaging hierarchy, campaign concept, and audience segmentation are the work that makes every downstream asset coherent, so don't hand them off. Build that layer with your team, then use AI to execute against it.

**Create a shared brief that travels.** The biggest coherence risk is several people prompting independently with no shared context. Put the positioning, voice rules, audience definition, and campaign objectives into one well-built brief, and use it as the input for every AI-assisted task. That dramatically reduces drift, and it makes it much easier to catch something that has gone off-brand.

**Establish visible quality standards.** The AI slop problem exists partly because the standard for "good enough" has shifted downward without anyone deciding that it should. Marketing leadership needs to spell out what good looks like and where AI output requires review, then keep to that standard even when the pressure to move fast is real.

None of this is complicated, but someone in the marketing function has to own it, and they need the credibility to have that conversation with the CEO and the Sales Director who are excited about the forty-minute deliverable.

For a B2B marketing leader right now, that is in many ways a more important capability than prompt engineering. It comes down to strategic clarity about where the value of the function lives, and the ability to defend it without sounding like you are defending the past.
